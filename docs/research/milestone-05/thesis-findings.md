**A thesis is a secondary source.** It reports documents I have not seen. Where it cites a deed, a gazette or a newspaper, the claim below is "the thesis reports that the document says", and the document's reference is given so it can be checked.

## The construction date

### Where the popular dates come from (pages 22 to 24)

| Date | Where the thesis traces it | The thesis's view |
|---|---|---|
| 1846 | A 1994 doctoral thesis by Marjorie Alonge, repeated in a 2017 Princeton thesis. Alonge's source was two interviews with one member of the Olaiya family | One oral source, unchecked |
| 1855 | Apparently Wikipedia, citing a 2002 book on tourism that the author could not find. "161 years old" in the press is the same date | Copied from the internet; origin lost |
| About 1880 | A 2015 photo book by the architect John Godwin and the photographer Gillian Hopwood | No basis given in the book |
| 1890 | One 2015 journal article, without a source | Unsupported |
| "About one hundred years ago", written in 1967 | Kenneth Murray, the first head of the colonial Antiquities Service | Where the official story started (page 59) |

Two experts consulted by the author thought the earliest dates unlikely on stylistic grounds (page 25). An architect writing in 1955, William Murray Jack, declined to date the building and remarked that the differing window patterns suggested it was built in two stages (page 22).

### What the Land Registry deeds show (pages 25 to 28)

The author found five deeds for the corner of Tinubu Square and Bamgbose Street. Several carry a sketch plan of the plot in the margin.

| Year | What the thesis reports the deed records | Registry reference given |
|---|---|---|
| 1890 | On 4 January, José Amoedo Fernandes, a trader, buys a plot on Tinubu Square. It covers only part of the later building's site | No 96, Vol. 16, p. 328 |
| 1895 | Vicente R. Guedes buys the adjoining plot at the Bamgbose Street corner. The sketch shows a house with a yard behind | No 37, Vol. 26, p. 134 |
| 1897 | Guedes has died. His mother, Maria dos Santos, and Fernandez agree that Fernandez will maintain the wall between their properties. The sketch marks a storey house on each plot | No 34, volume not legible, p. 120 |
| 1903 | On 5 May, dos Santos sells her plot to Fernandez. The whole site is in one person's hands for the first time. The yard is still drawn | No 28, Vol. 43, p. 90 |
| 1914 | Fernandez, by then back in Spain, sells the whole to Napoleon Rey Couto. The sketch now shows one built outline with no yard | No 113, Vol. 84, p. 423 |

**The author's conclusion:** the building as a whole was made between 1903 and 1914, by joining and extending two houses, the first of which went up between 1890 and 1897. She says plainly that whether the joining began before 1903 may never be known (page 38).

**How firm this is:** it is an inference from five deed sketches, not a building record. No document says "built in". It is far better grounded than any other date on offer.

## Who was involved

- **José Amoedo Fernandez** was a merchant from Fornelos de Montes, in Pontevedra, Galicia, Spain. The 1914 deed gives his origin (page 29). He was not a returnee from Brazil. He left Lagos in 1910 and died in Spain in 1938 (pages 39 and 41).
- **Vicente Rodrigues Guedes** was a Portuguese trader from Porto, and Fernandez's business partner in the early 1890s under the name Fernandes & Co (pages 33 and 34).
- **Napoleon Rey Couto**, also from Galicia, was Fernandez's later partner in Fernandez & Co, which sold wine and provisions from the square. He bought the building in 1914 and died in it on 9 March 1918 (pages 37, 39 to 41).
- **Alfred Omolana Olaiya**, a trader based in Calabar, bought it at auction on 17 December 1932 for £2,500. The deed is registered in 1933 (page 43). The thesis gives 1934 in two other places; I cannot tell which is a slip.
- **The building was never owned by Afro-Brazilian returnees** (page 43).
- **Who designed and built it is not documented** (page 44). The author argues that returnee craftsmen built it, from the style and from personal links between the owners and known builders. That is her inference, and she presents it as one.

## The name and the uses

- Daniel Olaiya, the buyer's eldest son, opened a restaurant and bar on the ground floor and named it Ilojo Bar (page 52).
- A restaurant in the 1930s, with a hotel alongside in the 1940s, and a music venue from the 1950s (page 52). A 1939 newspaper advertisement and an August 1946 magazine photograph are reproduced.
- Later, a musical-instrument shop run by the musician Victor Olaiya, until the 1990s (page 52).

## The monument

- **Declared a monument on 5 April 1956**, under the Antiquities Ordinance of 1953. The thesis cites Legal Notice 31 of 1956, Federation of Nigeria Official Gazette, 43:29 (page 54).
- **The notice names the property** as the building known as "Ilojo Bar", numbers 6 Alli Street and 2 Bamgbose Street, with its compound (page 54, note 208).
- In 1958 the Antiquities Department proposed buying it for a museum of old Lagos for the 1960 independence celebrations. The plan was dropped by May 1960; the archive does not say why (pages 56 to 58).
- **The official story that it was built by a family returned from Brazil first appears in that department's papers** of the 1950s and 1960s (page 59).

## The survey of 2012

- Early in 2012 the heritage group Legacy and GHK Architects, under John Godwin, scaffolded the front, **measured the building in detail** and tested its mortar (pages 63 and 64).
- A ground-floor plan from that measured drawing is reproduced, small, on page 26.
- The survey found the building badly decayed but structurally standing. A restoration was costed and never funded (pages 64 and 65).

## The demolition, as the thesis reports it

- A Lagos State planning authority issued a demolition permit on 29 April 2016. The federal monuments commission objected and the permit was withdrawn, but the withdrawal was sent to the wrong address (pages 67 and 68).
- **The building was demolished on Sunday 11 September 2016**, in about five hours, at the fourth attempt that year (page 69).
- The commission's banners on the building, warning that it was a protected monument, came down with it (page 69).
- The rooftop figure was removed beforehand and was shown again in public on 15 January 2021 (page 73).

Responsibility is disputed and was before the courts. The thesis gives its own view. **None of that belongs in the game**, which names no causes.

## The site afterwards

- A photograph by the author, captioned February 2021, shows the plot cleared: bare ground and rubble, stacked tyres, a few stalls, the exposed side wall of a neighbour, and a taller ochre building behind (page 71).
- A banner announcing a residential development went up after the demolition and was soon taken down (page 71).
- In January 2021 the family and the commission signed a memorandum, and an intention to reconstruct was announced (pages 73 and 89). I do not know what has happened since.

## The figure on the parapet

Locally called "the Angel". The thesis identifies it, from lettering on its base photographed in 2012, as a ceramic figure of Spring by a Porto maker, José Pereira Valente, and reports a specialist's opinion that it was made between 1884 and 1905 (pages 73 and 74).

## Three things this lets me work out

These are my inferences from putting the thesis beside the evidence already gathered.

### 1. The unequal bays match the two plots

On the thesis's reproduction of the 1914 sketch, the frontage to the square is annotated as 61 ft 1 in, made up of 26 ft 9 in and 34 ft 4 in. The image is small and I may have misread a digit, but the two parts do add up to the total.

- 34 ft 4 in divided by 26 ft 9 in is 1.28.
- In the Northwestern print, the three wide bays together measure 1.27 times the three narrow bays.

So the wide half of the front sits on the wider corner plot and the narrow half on Fernandez's original plot. **The front shows the join between the two properties.** The 1946 photograph puts the Bamgbose Street corner at the left end of the front, which is where the wide bays are in the print.

### 2. The front is narrower than I estimated

61 ft 1 in is 18.6 m. My estimate from people in the photograph was about 24 m. The deed figure is the better one. By the same proportions the height to the parapet would be about 10 m, not 12 to 13 m. The 2012 measured drawings would settle both.

### 3. The Northwestern print is probably from the later 1940s or the 1950s

It shows both an "ILOJO HOTEL" sign, which is in the August 1946 photograph, and an "OJOBARO BROTHERS" sign, which is in Hopwood's photograph from the 1950s. That narrows it from four decades to perhaps one or two. It is still not a date.
